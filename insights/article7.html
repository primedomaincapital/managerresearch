How Much Does Manager Research Cost an Allocator?

A full manager-research program costs anywhere from $100,000 a year to several million. The bill is four separate charges — staff, databases, consultants, and the outsourced CIO — and most allocators only ever see two of them.

Prime Domain Capital · Market Intelligence

In the spring of 2026, the finance committee of a Midwestern university endowment sat down to answer a question nobody in the room had ever priced. The endowment, about $900 million, paid its external managers roughly $6 million a year in fees. It paid its consultant a retainer. It paid for two database seats and a research analyst. What it had never done was add those numbers together and ask what the research function itself cost. When the chief investment officer did the addition on a whiteboard, the total was more than double what anyone in the room had guessed — and the research budget, it turned out, had never once been discussed as a budget at all.

That is the normal state of affairs. Manager research is the one line in an allocator’s budget that is never presented as a line. It arrives as four separate bills, paid from four different budgets, approved by four different people — and because no one sees the total, no one asks whether the total is buying anything. The four bills are staff, databases, consultants, and, for the institutions that have given up on the first three, the outsourced CIO. Priced separately, each looks modest. Priced together, they are the second-largest cost in the investment program, after the managers themselves.

The first bill: people

The largest line is the one most allocators never count, because it sits in the salary budget rather than the investment budget. The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics put the national median annual wage for financial and investment analysts at $102,740 in May 2025, with the 90th percentile at $180,860 — and the median in the Bridgeport-Stamford-Danbury metro area, where much of the industry sits, at $158,310. Those are base wages, before benefits, bonus, or overhead. A derived employer-cost estimate built from the Bureau’s compensation data puts the all-in cost of a median analyst at roughly $154,000 a year, before any bonus. A two-person research team, in other words, costs on the order of $300,000 a year before it buys a single database or makes a single trip — and a senior research head at a large pension or endowment costs far more than the median.

That is the floor, not the ceiling. A research team that covers private markets needs people who can read a private equity track record, which commands a premium over a generalist. And the headcount math is unforgiving: a due diligence questionnaire takes an analyst two to five days to read properly, according to the manager-selection guide published by V7 Labs — multiply that by twenty candidates and the question stops being scheduling and becomes staffing. The institutions that do this work well employ research teams of five, ten, twenty people. The institutions that do it badly employ one person and a database login.

The second bill: the databases

The databases are the cheapest line and the easiest to price, because one vendor actually publishes a number. Morningstar disclosed in 2016 that Morningstar Direct cost $17,000 a year for the first user, $10,500 for the second, and $9,000 for each additional user; third-party pricing trackers now put the first seat at about $17,500. That figure is a decade old and should be read as a floor, but it is the only published institutional price in the category. Three seats of Direct, at the published schedule, run about $37,000 a year.

Everything else is a quote, not a price. Nasdaq eVestment, the default screen for traditional and hedge fund searches, does not publish pricing; the Dakota database guide for 2024 estimated it at $15,000 to $60,000 a year. Preqin, the default for alternatives and now part of BlackRock after its $3.2 billion acquisition, is estimated by the same guide at $5,000 to $30,000, while a separate pricing tracker put the median Preqin contract near $50,000 a year, with entry configurations around $25,000. A Bloomberg Terminal, for the shops that run one alongside the research databases, was checked at $31,980 per seat per year for a single seat in August 2026 by the pricing tracker Investables.ai. All of those figures carry the same caveat: they are third-party estimates of negotiated prices, not vendor price lists, and the real number arrives after a sales conversation. The honest summary is that a mid-size allocator should budget $50,000 to $150,000 a year for data before it hires anyone to read it.

The third bill: the consultant

The consultant’s fee is the only one that is a matter of public record, because registered advisers must file it. IPEX, a boutique consulting firm, disclosed in its Form ADV filed with the SEC in March 2026 that its annual retainer for new clients typically runs $10,000 to $100,000, with project fees of $5,000 to $30,000 — and its maximum fee schedule runs from 100 basis points on the first $5 million down to 25 basis points above $50 million. Oakmont Capital Management, which merged with BilkeyKatz Investment Consultants in 2025, disclosed a fixed annual fee of $50,000 to $500,000 for its outsourced-CIO and consulting relationships. Those two filings bracket the market for small and mid-size institutions: five figures for advice, six figures once the relationship covers the whole portfolio.

The large-institution numbers are older but instructive. Allan Martin, a managing partner at NEPC, told Financial Advisor magazine that a $1 billion to $5 billion institution might pay one or two basis points a year — about $500,000 on a $5 billion portfolio — to retain a generalist due-diligence firm, while specialists focused on a single asset class typically charged 10 to 20 basis points on the committed assets. That interview is from around 2010 and the market has moved since, but the shape of it has not: generalist advice is cheap in basis points and expensive in dollars, and specialist advice is expensive in both. The British evidence points the same way. The UK Competition and Markets Authority, in its 2018 investigation of investment consultants, found that median fiduciary management fees were around five times median investment consulting fees — advice alone was about a tenth of the combined advice-and-management bill. The consultant is the cheapest way to buy research, and the price reflects exactly how much research you are getting.

The fourth bill: handing it over

The fourth bill is the one that replaces the other three. An outsourced chief investment officer takes the research function, the manager selection, and the discretion, and charges for all of it in basis points. The range most often quoted in the industry is 0.25% to 0.65% of assets, from a panel discussion reported in CIO magazine and summarised on Wikipedia’s investment-outsourcing page; endowment-style outsourcers run higher, 0.35% to 1.5%, often with an incentive fee on top. On a $500 million portfolio, the middle of that range is $1.25 million to $3.25 million a year — every bill above, rolled into one, plus the discretion to act on it. Cerulli Associates put U.S. OCIO assets at $3.3 trillion at the end of 2024 and projected $5.6 trillion by 2029, growth of about 10.6% a year. The institutions buying it are, in effect, deciding that the research function is worth more than the sum of its parts, and paying accordingly.

What the totals look like

Built from the component prices above — not taken from any survey, because no vendor-neutral survey of total research cost exists — the bands fall out like this. A small allocator, a $100 million family office or foundation with one part-time research professional, one Morningstar Direct seat, and one database subscription, should plan on $100,000 to $250,000 a year. A mid-size institution, a $1 billion pension or endowment with two analysts, three database seats, two subscriptions, and a consultant retainer, lands at $500,000 to $1 million a year. A large allocator, a $10 billion-plus fund with a research team of five to eight, the full data stack, a consultant, and an OCIO-style oversight layer, should expect $2 million to $5 million a year. The bands are deliberately wide, because the variable that moves them most is a decision, not a price: how much of the work you do yourself.

The per-decision arithmetic is what makes the bands worth arguing about. A mid-size institution that hires four managers a year on a $750,000 research budget is paying about $190,000 per hiring decision. A small allocator that hires one manager every two years on a $150,000 budget is paying $300,000 per decision. Research is cheap when it changes a decision and ruinously expensive when it does not — which is why the budget question and the quality question are the same question.

The honest counterargument

There is a serious case for spending much less, and it starts with the record of the people being paid. The UK Competition and Markets Authority found that consultant recommendations underperformed simple benchmarks often enough to make the fee itself a live question, and the same pattern shows up in the U.S. data. A database subscription plus a disciplined investment committee can replicate much of what a retainer buys, at a tenth of the price. The consultants’ own defence is that the fee buys process, governance, and someone to blame, which is true and is also not research.

The counterargument fails only at the point where the work gets hard. Databases rank the past. A consultant’s memo can be skimmed. The part of manager research that actually prevents losses — the operational review, the capacity analysis, the reference call to the former employer — is labour, and labour is what the budget buys. Spend less than the work costs and the work does not get cheaper; it gets skipped.

What to do with the number

Price the function, not the subscription. Add the salaries, the databases, the retainer, and the OCIO fee into one number, present it to the board as one number, and ask whether the decisions it produced in the last three years were worth it. If the answer is yes, the number is probably too low — good research is the cheapest insurance in the portfolio. If the answer is no, the fix is rarely to cut the budget; it is to cut the parts of the program that never changed a decision, which are usually the most expensive parts. The institutions that get this right treat manager research as a cost of being in the business of allocating capital, alongside custody and audit. The institutions that get it wrong treat it as an overhead to be minimised, until a manager hired in three weeks costs them more than a decade of research budgets.
