Active’s Best Year in a While Still Leaves 60% Behind

Morningstar’s mid-2026 Barometer shows active funds gaining ground — just over 40% beat passive over one year. Over ten years, only 25% did. The allocator’s job is knowing which 40% is which.

Prime Domain Capital · Market Intelligence

In August 2026, an investment committee stared at the newest Morningstar Active/Passive Barometer and found the most encouraging one-year numbers for active management in years — sitting next to a ten-year record that still says three in four active strategies fail. Both numbers were true. Both numbers were from the same study. The committee’s job was to decide which one to act on.

The study is the largest of its kind. Morningstar’s US Active/Passive Barometer, midyear 2026 edition, spans nearly 9,226 unique funds accounting for approximately $29 trillion in assets, or about 67% of the US fund market as of the end of June 2026. Over the twelve months through June 2026, just over 40% of active funds survived and beat their asset-weighted average passive composite — an increase of 7 percentage points from a year earlier. US stock-pickers reached a 38% success rate, up 4 points. But over the ten years through June 2026, just 25% of active strategies survived and beat their passive counterparts. Morningstar’s summary of the midyear 2026 Barometer carries the headline figures; Wealth Professional’s report on the study notes the same split: meaningful short-term gains, stubborn long-term shortfall.

The wins were not spread evenly. Active small-cap managers led the improvement at a 49% success rate, mid-cap managers at 47%, while large-cap managers detracted at 27%. International stock managers held steady at 44%. Active diversified emerging-market funds had the second-highest success rate in the study at 70%, a 35-percentage-point increase from last year. The large-cap number is the one that matters most to allocators, because large-cap is where most of the money sits — and where active has the worst record. In large-cap growth, the hardest ground in the market, just 5% of active funds survived and beat their passive benchmark over ten years.

Where active won outright, the pattern is instructive. Active bond managers’ success rates shot up 22 percentage points to 52% over the twelve months through June 2026, with intermediate-core bond managers leading at 66%. Active real estate fund success rates rose 36 percentage points to 61%, and active global real estate funds recorded the highest one-year success rate of any category studied at 73.7%, up from just 15% a year earlier. The wins cluster where benchmarks are hardest to replicate cheaply, where markets are least efficient, or where a bad year for the category flattered the rebound.

The study’s most useful finding for allocators is about fees, not skill. Over the ten years through June 2026, 33% of active funds in the cheapest quintile of their categories beat their average passive peer, compared with 20% for the priciest funds. And the average dollar invested in active funds outperformed the average active fund in 16 of the 20 categories examined — investors, in aggregate, picked better than random. The Barometer was authored by Bryan Armour, director of passive strategies research at Morningstar Manager Research, with Brendan McCann and Brian Paoli.

The question was never whether active management can win. It is whether you can tell, in advance, which funds will — and whether the fee leaves anything worth having.

The honest counterargument

One-year success rates are cyclical, and 2026’s improvement has a cyclical smell. The bond rebound followed an abysmal 2025 for active bond funds; the small- and mid-cap value strength that drove the equity gain is a factor rotation as much as a skill story; and “success” in this study means surviving and beating, so fund closures do half the damage in the ten-year numbers. A single good year does not repeal the ten-year record — and the ten-year record says that an allocator picking an active fund at random faces a 60% probability of underperformance in year one and a 75% probability over a decade.

There is also a selection problem in reading the study as a buying guide. The categories where active won — real estate, emerging-market debt, small-cap value — are precisely the categories where capacity is limited and the best funds close to new investors. The 73.7% success rate in global real estate is a fact about the past twelve months, not an invitation.

What the research actually says to do

The Barometer supports a narrow, disciplined use of active management rather than a broad one. Allocate active where it has won repeatedly — bonds, real estate, small- and mid-cap — and index the large-cap core where the ten-year success rate is 5% in growth and 27% overall. Buy the cheapest quartile, because the fee finding is the most robust in the study. Judge on ten-year survival-adjusted numbers, not one-year headlines. And treat a one-year win as a data point, not a thesis: the same study showed a 7-point improvement in one year and a 75% failure rate over ten, and both numbers describe the same managers.

Manager research is a fee-and-category discipline, not a stock-picking contest. The 2026 Barometer gives allocators permission to use active management — narrowly, cheaply, and where the evidence is. It does not give them permission to forget why the permission is narrow.
