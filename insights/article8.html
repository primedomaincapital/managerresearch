How Long Should Manager Due Diligence Take Before an Allocation?

Three to six months is the honest answer for most mandates, six to eighteen for a first-time fund. The calendar is set by document review, on-site visits, reference checks, and operational diligence — and the shortcuts that save weeks are the ones that produce the worst mistakes.

Prime Domain Capital · Market Intelligence

In the autumn of 2024, an investment committee approved a new credit manager three weeks after the first meeting. The returns were strong, the fee was fair, the pitch book was immaculate, and the consultant’s memo was four pages of unqualified praise. Fourteen months later the fund gated redemptions, the administrator restated two quarters of valuations, and the allocator discovered — from a newspaper — that the firm’s chief operating officer had resigned six months before the allocation. Nothing in the three-week process had been designed to find any of that. The process had been designed to end.

That is the failure mode of fast diligence — and, paradoxically, of slow diligence done badly: a year of meetings that never asks the question the allocation turns on. The honest answer to how long due diligence should take is three to six months for most mandates, six to eighteen months for a first-time fund, and four to eight weeks for a hedge fund whose documentation is already in order. The point is what each stage of the calendar is for — and which stages get skipped when it is compressed.

The honest answer, by mandate

The averages cluster tightly enough to plan around. The manager-selection guide published by V7 Labs puts a full cycle, from due diligence questionnaire to investment committee decision, at three to six months for a private equity fund, and four to eight weeks for a hedge fund with comprehensive documentation already prepared. Dasseti, the due-diligence workflow firm now part of Nasdaq eVestment, puts the average manager selection at three to six months, with some processes running over a year and up to thirty-six months. For a first-time fund, the planning range is longer: six to eighteen months from first contact to a funded commitment, according to the institutional due-diligence benchmarks compiled by Investor Ready Capital, and up to twenty-four months with large pensions or endowments establishing a new manager relationship. Better Society Capital, the UK social-impact investor, says its own due diligence stage takes two to six months, with onboarding adding one to four months more.

A hedge fund with audited financials and a standard administrator can be diligenced in weeks, because most of the verification already exists in documents other people produced. A first-time private markets fund cannot, because the documents do not exist yet and the allocator is underwriting people, not a record. The allocators who get into trouble apply the hedge fund calendar to a private markets mandate, or the private markets calendar to a liquid strategy whose capacity will be gone by the time the committee meets.

Stage one: the documents

Everything starts with paper, and paper is slower than anyone budgets for. The Institutional Limited Partners Association’s due diligence questionnaire, the ILPA DDQ 2.0, runs to 250 or more questions. Reading one manager’s completed package properly takes an analyst somewhere between two and five days, according to V7 Labs. The back-and-forth that follows, as the allocator asks for the audited financials, the compliance manual, the valuation policy, and the answers the DDQ glossed over, typically stretches the document cycle to four to six weeks, according to CENTRL, the due-diligence workflow platform.

The document stage is where the first selection error is made — by speed. An allocator that reads twenty DDQs in a week has not done twenty diligences; it has done twenty skim-reads. The questionnaire is a map of where to probe, not the probe itself — AIMA, which publishes the standard hedge fund DDQ, calls it “an early step in the due diligence process.” The dig is the rest of the calendar.

Stage two: the visit

The on-site visit is the stage most often cut, and the one that catches the most. A visit is not a meeting; it is an inspection. The allocator sits with the portfolio managers, meets the operations staff the pitch book never mentions, and looks at the systems — the order management, the risk reports, the valuation workflow — rather than the slides about them. A visit that is done properly takes a day on site and a week of preparation and follow-up.

Virtual visits have made this stage cheaper and, in the worst cases, thinner. A video call can confirm that a team exists and can answer questions. It cannot confirm who actually runs the portfolio, whether the office the manager claims is the office the manager occupies, or whether the person answering the risk questions is the person who built the risk system. The allocators who learned this the hard way now treat an on-site visit as a condition of investment, not a preference.

Stage three: the references

Reference checks are the cheapest stage and the most frequently faked. The manager supplies three names, all of whom will say lovely things. The check that matters is the one the manager did not supply: the former employer, the administrator who used to service the fund, the allocator who redeemed. A proper reference round takes two to four weeks, because the people worth talking to are busy, because the candid ones want the call off the record, and because one call always produces the name of another person who should be called. The question that separates a real reference check from a ritual is the one about what went wrong — and the answer is only useful if the allocator already knows enough from the documents to recognise an evasion.

Background checks run alongside, and they are not a formality. The standard checks — regulatory history, litigation, employment verification — take days. The useful ones — speaking to people who worked with the principals in a previous firm — take weeks, and they surface the pattern a database never will.

Stage four: operational due diligence

Operational due diligence is the stage that kills the most allocations, and the stage most often compressed into an afternoon. The benchmarks put it at six to ten weeks as a standalone track, running in parallel with the investment review; Carta says it can run from a few weeks for highly organised firms to several months where auditors must untangle manual spreadsheets. The scope is the eight areas where money can be lost without the strategy being at fault: fund structure and service providers, custody and cash controls, valuation, the audit, controls reports, regulatory and legal history, key people and governance, and continuity and cyber resilience. The method is verification against sources the manager does not control — the administrator, the custodian, the auditor, the regulator — rather than the manager’s own answers.

The Standards Board for Alternative Investments gives operational due diligence teams the right to veto an investment outright, and the veto is used. The allocators who fold ODD into the investment review, or who let the investment team overrule it, have not saved six weeks. They have spent six weeks of investment work on a manager their own operations team would have rejected.

Stage five: the committee

The final stage is the one nobody controls. The investment memo takes four to twelve weeks to write, circulate, and approve, according to the institutional benchmarks. Most investment committees meet quarterly. A memo that misses the March meeting by a week waits until June, and the manager that was available in March may not be available in June. This is the hidden tax on slow diligence: a process that takes four months of work can take seven months of calendar.

Legal and onboarding add their own month. Better Society Capital budgets one to four months from agreement in principle to signed documents, and that is for a cooperative manager with prepared paperwork.

The shortcuts that produce the worst errors

The history of manager selection is, to a remarkable degree, a history of skipped stages. Bernard Madoff ran the largest fraud in the industry’s history — a Ponzi scheme that cost investors an estimated $17 billion in principal — and the feeder funds that channelled money to him are the case study in what compressed diligence looks like. Fairfield Greenwich, at one point managing about $14 billion, had nearly $7 billion invested with Madoff through its flagship funds. In April 2009, the Massachusetts Secretary of the Commonwealth accused Fairfield Greenwich Advisors of “complete disregard of its fiduciary duties,” alleging the firm “did not engage in meaningful due diligence” and “turned a blind eye to any fact that would have burst their lucrative bubble.” The red flags were not hidden: Madoff’s auditor, Friehling & Horowitz, was a three-person firm working out of a 13-by-18-foot office; his returns could not be replicated by other managers’ quantitative analysts; he initiated the trades, executed them, and administered the assets himself, with no segregation of duties. Harry Markopolos had been warning the SEC about Madoff since 2000. Madoff was sentenced to 150 years in prison in June 2009.

The pattern is ordinary, and the academic evidence points the same way: Brown, Goetzmann, Liang, and Schwarz, in their 2009 paper “Trust and Delegation,” built on 444 hedge fund due diligence reports written between 2003 and 2008, found that the operational risks diligence is supposed to catch — the ones that have nothing to do with the investment strategy — were the ones that predicted failure. Every shortcut maps to a stage: skipping the visit means never seeing the operations; skipping the references means never hearing from the people who left; skipping ODD means trusting the manager’s word about the manager’s controls.

The honest counterargument

Speed has real value, and the case for moving fast deserves a fair hearing. Capacity closes: the best managers in the categories where active management actually wins — small-cap, real estate, emerging markets — close to new investors, and a six-month process can mean watching the door shut. And a process that drags to thirty-six months, as Dasseti notes some do, has its own cost: the allocation never happens.

The counterargument is right about the cost of waiting and wrong about what waiting buys. The stages that take the time — the document verification, the visit, the references, the operational review — are the stages that produce the information. A faster process that skips them is not faster diligence; it is worse diligence. The right response to a closing fund is not to skip the visit. It is to start earlier and run the tracks in parallel.

What the calendar is for

Budget the calendar the way you budget the fee. A three-to-six-month process is not a delay; it is the price of knowing what you are buying, and it is cheap next to the cost of unwinding a bad allocation — the gated fund, the restated valuations, the litigation. Run the tracks in parallel, not in sequence, so the calendar is set by the longest track rather than the sum of all of them. Put the operational review on a separate track with a veto, running from the shortlist stage. And treat the committee date as a deadline to work backwards from — a memo that misses the quarter by a week costs three months.
