OCIO vs. In-House: The Manager Research Debate

US OCIO assets hit $3.3T in 2024 and are headed for $5.6T by 2029. The case for outsourcing the CIO function — and the honest case for keeping it in-house.

Prime Domain Capital · Market Intelligence

The fastest-growing job in institutional investing is the one that does not exist inside the institution. Outsourced chief investment officer mandates — the full delegation of portfolio strategy, manager selection, and implementation to an external fiduciary — have gone from a niche solution for small endowments to one of the industry's defining structural shifts. Cerulli Associates puts US OCIO assets at $3.3 trillion at the end of 2024, tripled in less than a decade, and projects $5.6 trillion by 2029 — a 10.6% annual growth rate. Chestnut Advisory's global census counted $2.5 trillion in the US alone in 2025, up 16% in a single year, and forecasts $5.78 trillion globally by 2030. The five largest providers — Mercer at roughly $670 billion, Goldman Sachs at $411 billion, BlackRock at $372 billion, Russell Investments at $355 billion — now oversee sums that rival mid-sized sovereign wealth funds. This is no longer an experiment. It is a reorganization of who makes investment decisions.

The client list tells you why. The model first took hold among small and mid-sized institutions without the scale for a full investment office. Since the pandemic, the adoption curve has moved decisively upmarket: large defined-benefit plans with established internal teams — BAE Systems' £23 billion scheme, the Royal Mail and Centrica pension plans, the National Grid scheme — have all made the shift. When investors with the resources to do it themselves choose not to, the question stops being whether OCIO works and becomes what the in-house model is actually for.

Why the OCIO Wave Is Accelerating

The drivers are structural, not cyclical, which is why the growth keeps compounding through market regimes. First, complexity: the average institutional portfolio now spans private equity, private credit, real assets, hedge funds, and co-investments alongside public markets — each with its own diligence demands, fee negotiations, and liquidity management. A five-person internal team cannot cover that opportunity set with professional depth. An OCIO provider with hundreds of investment professionals can, and the cost is shared across clients.

Second, governance. Chestnut's survey work found that 75% of institutional investors rank governance advisory as the most important factor in OCIO hiring decisions — ahead of performance. That surprises people who think this is about returns; it should not. Investment committees meet quarterly, turn over regularly, and are asked to oversee portfolios of staggering complexity. The OCIO model replaces episodic oversight with continuous professional management and gives the board a single accountable fiduciary. For trustees with personal liability, that accountability has a value no return stream captures.

Third, the talent economics have inverted. Hiring and retaining a full investment office — CIO, asset-class heads, operations, risk — costs millions annually before a dollar is managed, and the competition for that talent is the same OCIO providers doing the hiring. For any institution below roughly $5–10 billion, the math of building versus buying has quietly become indefensible: you cannot hire the team the complexity requires at a cost the portfolio can bear.

Fourth, speed. Manager selection, tactical allocation, and implementation happen continuously under OCIO, not at quarterly committee cadence. In volatile markets, the ability to act in days rather than quarters is a genuine source of value — and a genuine source of risk, which brings us to the other side.

The Honest Case for Keeping It In-House

The OCIO skeptics have real arguments, and the strongest ones are about alignment and control. An OCIO provider is a business, and its economics reward asset retention and cross-selling as surely as any asset manager's. The conflicts are well-documented: providers that manufacture their own funds have an incentive to fill client portfolios with them, and the industry's fee structures — often basis points on assets plus performance overlays — can create precisely the agency problems the model claims to solve. Diligence on the OCIO provider itself, including its fund-affiliation biases and its actual (not model) portfolio results, is as demanding as any manager search. Outsourcing the CIO function does not outsource the fiduciary duty; the board remains responsible, now with an added layer to monitor.

There is also the customization argument. The largest and most sophisticated asset owners — the sovereign funds, the mega-endowments — keep investment in-house because their scale supports it and their missions demand bespoke portfolios no commingled OCIO solution can replicate. If your institution has genuine scale, a stable governance structure, and a CIO worth retaining, the in-house model preserves something OCIO cannot sell: institutional memory, mission alignment, and direct control over every allocation decision. The OCIO industry's own growth is concentrated below the mega-institution tier for a reason.

And a quieter risk: herding. As OCIO consolidates — the investment consulting industry has shrunk from more than 100 firms in 2010 to 58 in 2025, with the top five consultants advising on roughly 70% of worldwide institutional assets — more capital gets managed through fewer models. Correlated advice across hundreds of institutions is a systemic feature nobody signed up for. When the consensus OCIO portfolio tilts the same way, the tilt itself becomes the risk.

The Decision Framework

So how should a board decide? Four questions, in order.

One: can we hire and retain the team the portfolio's complexity requires, at a sustainable cost? If the honest answer is no, the debate is over — the only question is which provider.

Two: what is the true all-in cost comparison? Model the internal office fully loaded (compensation, systems, consultants, travel, board time) against the OCIO fee net of the investment cost savings the provider's scale delivers (fee negotiations with underlying managers are a real and underappreciated source of value).

Three: where do we add value as a board? If the board's edge is mission, spending policy, and risk tolerance — not manager selection — then delegating implementation to professionals and focusing governance on what the board uniquely owns is the higher-value use of trustee time.

Four: can we be a good client? The OCIO model fails when boards delegate and disengage, then second-guess every drawdown. It works when the board sets clear objectives, monitors the provider rigorously, and lets the professionals work.

The quotable bottom line: the OCIO question was never "is outsourcing better" — it is "better at what, for whom, and at what price." For most institutions below mega-scale, the honest answer increasingly favors delegation. For the few with true scale and genuine investment capability, keeping it in-house remains the right call. The worst outcome is the muddle in the middle: an under-resourced internal team making under-informed decisions at committee pace, paying active fees for passive outcomes. That is the model the OCIO wave is actually displacing — and good riddance.
