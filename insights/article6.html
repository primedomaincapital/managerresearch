Investment Manager Research: What It Is, How Allocators Use It, and Who Provides It

Investment manager research is the discipline behind every allocation decision: the databases, the due diligence, and the judgment that separates a manager worth backing from one worth passing on.

Prime Domain Capital · Market Intelligence

Two finalists, one mandate. An investment committee in Chicago spent a Tuesday afternoon with both. Same ten-year return, same fee, same polished presentation. The difference was not on the tear sheet. One manager ran $4 billion across 40 accounts from a team that had worked together for a decade. The other ran $18 billion across 400 accounts, had lost its head of research six months earlier, and was running the strategy at three times the capacity its own prospectus had once called its limit. The committee hired the first manager. The second manager’s returns, a year later, were still fine. The decision was still right. Investment manager research is the work that makes that distinction possible — and the work most allocators do last, if they do it at all.

What investment manager research actually is

Investment manager research is the discipline of finding, vetting, and monitoring the firms to whom institutions entrust capital. It has three parts, and only the first is glamorous. Sourcing is the database work: screening thousands of managers by strategy, size, track record, and fees. Diligence is the human work: meeting the team, testing the investment process, auditing operations, and checking whether the story the pitch book tells matches the business the firm actually runs. Monitoring is the unglamorous work: watching for the drift, the departures, and the capacity creep that turn a good hire into a bad holding. An investment manager database is the tool for the first part. It is not a substitute for the other two, and the firms that forget that distinction are the ones that hire well and hold badly.

The stakes are set by the base rate. In 2025, 79% of large-cap managers lagged the S&P 500, and zero top-quartile funds repeated their ranking through the year. Morningstar’s mid-2026 Barometer found just over 40% of active funds beating passive over one year, the best showing in a while, which still leaves 60% behind. The research question is never whether a manager beat the market last year. It is whether the manager has a repeatable reason to beat it next year — and whether the allocator can tell the difference between the two.

The databases behind the work

An investment manager database is a structured record of the manager universe: returns, assets under management, holdings, personnel, fees, and strategy classifications, collected across thousands of firms and updated continuously. The global investment manager database is not one product but a small set of them, and the names recur in every allocator’s workflow. eVestment, the institutional database now owned by Nasdaq, is the default screen for traditional long-only and hedge fund searches. Morningstar Direct is the default for fund-level analysis and peer comparison. Preqin is the default for alternatives — private equity, private credit, real assets — where the data is thinner and the manager universe is harder to map. Burgiss covers private markets performance and benchmarking, the numbers behind the allocation decision in illiquid strategies.

Manager research database providers sell comprehensiveness, and they mostly deliver it. What they cannot sell is judgment. A database will tell you a manager returned 12% a year for a decade. It will not tell you the returns were earned by one person who has since left, or that the strategy’s capacity was breached two years ago, or that the firm’s largest client is redeeming. The database ranks the past. The allocator’s job is to price the future — and the research that matters most is the research the database cannot do.

The professional manager research platform is the layer on top: the workflow that turns database screens into shortlists, shortlists into diligence, and diligence into decisions. The platforms differ in coverage and price, but the buying question is the same for all of them: does the platform make the research better, or does it make the research faster? Faster is worth paying for. Better is worth much more, and it is much rarer.

How allocators use it in decisions

How do institutional allocators use manager research in decisions? As the filter before the decision, not the decision itself. The research sets the terms on which a manager can be picked: the operational checks that rule out firms with hidden fragility, the capacity analysis that rules out strategies that cannot absorb the allocation, and the incentive analysis that rules out teams whose economics point the wrong way. Performance earns the meeting. Manager research decides whether the meeting should have happened at all.

The same logic explains the structural shift toward outsourcing the function entirely. US OCIO assets hit $3.3 trillion at the end of 2024 and are headed for $5.6 trillion by 2029 — institutions buying the research function rather than building it. Investment consultants, who influence some $25 trillion in institutional assets, are the older version of the same trade: rented research, bought by institutions that cannot staff it themselves. The question in each case is identical, and it is the question manager research exists to answer: who picks the managers, and how do you know they are any good at it?

Where it breaks down

The honest counterargument is that manager research has a poor record of its own. Database returns are self-reported, and the managers who stop reporting are usually the ones with something to hide — survivorship bias that flatters every peer group it touches. Backfill bias does the same work from the other side: a manager’s early years enter the database only once the record looks good enough to show. The consultants whose recommendations allocators pay for have underperformed the simple alternatives often enough that the fee itself is a live question. And the deepest failure is conceptual: research that ranks managers by past returns is measuring the one thing about a manager that is guaranteed not to repeat.

None of that makes the research worthless. It makes it a discipline rather than a product — a set of questions asked in the right order, by people with the authority to act on the answers. The database finds the candidates. The diligence kills most of them. The monitoring catches the rest before they cost real money. An allocator that treats manager research as a subscription will get a subscription’s worth of value. An allocator that treats it as the core of the investment function will get the only edge in manager selection that has ever existed: knowing more about the manager than the manager’s own pitch book does.
