Manager Databases vs. Consultant Shortlists: Where Allocators Actually Find Managers

A keyword search for "growth equity" in a manager database returns three to five thousand managers. A consultant's shortlist for the same mandate has ten to twenty names. Both are answers to the same question — who should manage this money — and they are answers of entirely different kinds. The database tells an allocator what exists. The shortlist tells an allocator what survives. The difference between the two is where manager research actually happens, and it is larger than most allocators admit.

Prime Domain Capital · Market Intelligence

The databases are enormous, and they are free to the people who use them. Nasdaq's eVestment — founded in 2000 by former Towers Watson executives — lists more than 2,700 managers and more than 30,000 strategies, with 25,092 manager ratings, 76,616 consultant-authored documents, and more than $90 trillion of assets represented across 1,000-plus organizations in 60-plus countries. AlternativeSoft aggregates more than 500,000 funds from over 15 data providers. Mercer, Preqin, PitchBook, Morningstar Direct, and Hedge Fund Research add their own universes on top. The allocators who query them pay nothing; the managers who want to be found pay $200,000 to $300,000 a year or more for the privilege of listing. That is the business model, and it shapes everything that follows: the database is a directory whose customers are the managers, not the allocators.

What the database actually returns

For the allocator, the database is the most-used due-diligence tool in the industry — and the most misleading one if it is used alone. The problem is not the size of the universe. It is the quality of the sample. Databases suffer from survivorship bias: funds that fail stop reporting, so the universe quietly sheds its worst performers. Research by Ferguson-McGuire found that 18.2% of funds were dropped from databases, against only 6.5% that reported poor performance, and roughly 30% of funds had missing returns. A separate study by Burgos and co-authors found that 10% to 20% of funds understate their returns in database submissions. The database does not lie. It simply shows the funds that chose to be shown, in the numbers they chose to report.

The second problem is coverage. Databases capture the managers who pay to be listed and who report consistently. They underrepresent the managers who do not need to — the small emerging funds, the capacity-constrained strategies, the managers who raise by referral. That is a real gap, because emerging managers are not a rounding error: they hold about 20% of private equity assets, account for 54% of top-quartile funds, and 68% of the 18,000-plus institutional investors surveyed by Ferguson-McGuire in 2024 are actively seeking them. The database is deepest where the managers are largest and most established, and shallowest exactly where the outperformance has historically been found.

The third problem is the search itself. A database returns what matches the query. It does not return what the allocator should have asked for. A keyword search for a strategy retrieves thousands of names with no way to distinguish the manager whose returns came from skill from the one whose returns came from leverage, or the one whose track record is real from the one whose track record was assembled from a predecessor fund's results. The database gives the allocator a universe. It does not give the allocator a thesis.

What the shortlist adds — and what it costs

The consultant's shortlist is the answer to those three problems, and it is priced accordingly. A manager search through a consultant runs $30,000 to $150,000, against a database subscription the allocator does not pay for. For that fee, the consultant screens a hundred or more managers, applies the allocator's constraints — size, strategy, fee, governance, track record length — and returns ten to twenty names that have already passed an initial operational and investment screen. Roughly 40% to 50% of manager searches involve consultant input, and one in four allocators relies heavily on consultants specifically for emerging-manager identification. Consultant research databases account for about half of all databases used in the industry, and eVestment is the most-used of them — which means the consultant is often querying the same database the allocator could query directly, and adding judgment the allocator cannot buy separately.

The judgment is real, and it is also the shortlist's weakness. A consultant's shortlist reflects the consultant's relationships, its approved-manager list, and its own incentives — which include the fee it earns for the search and, in some models, an ongoing relationship with the manager it recommends. The shortlist is narrower than the database by design, and narrowness is a risk: the manager who was not on the approved list is not on the shortlist, however good the returns. The consultant also moves slowly. Due diligence on a first-time fund runs six to eighteen months and costs $30,000 to $100,000 per manager. A shortlist of ten managers is a year of work.

Where allocators actually find managers

The honest answer is that allocators find managers in both places, and the split follows the mandate. For large, established strategies — core fixed income, large-cap equity, the mainstream hedge fund categories — the database is sufficient, and the allocator's own team does the work. For emerging managers, capacity-constrained strategies, and first-time funds, the consultant's network matters more, because the database does not cover those managers well and the allocator cannot diligence them alone. AlternativeSoft's own client base illustrates the split: about 20% of its clients work with a consultant, and 80% are self-directed. The self-directed majority is not wrong. It is simply working in the part of the market where the database is deepest.

The counterargument deserves its weight, because the database's defenders have a point. The consultant model is expensive, slow, and conflicted — and the data shows that the best-performing managers are often the ones the consultants do not cover. A disciplined allocator with a small team can build a better process on the database alone: screen on quantitative criteria, demand audited returns, verify the track record against the administrator's records (about 95% of hedge fund assets sit with independent administrators, so the verification is straightforward), and run the operational diligence in-house. The $30,000 to $150,000 consultant fee buys a shortlist that the allocator's own analyst could have built in a month. For a large allocator with an internal research team, that is a strong argument.

But the argument has a limit, and the limit is time. An allocator's internal team can screen a database. It cannot visit ten managers, check ten sets of references, and review ten operational due-diligence questionnaires in a quarter. The consultant's value is not the list. It is the labor. The allocator who replaces the consultant with the database is not saving $100,000. It is moving the work onto its own staff, at its own opportunity cost, and hoping the staff has the experience to spot the fraud the consultant would have caught. The 2023 collapse of a well-known fund that had passed database screens but failed operational diligence is the cautionary tale here — and it is the reason the consultant's operational review, not the investment recommendation, is the part of the service that is hardest to replace.

For an allocator, the practical conclusion is to use the database for the universe and the consultant for the shortlist — and to keep the two functions separate. Query the database directly, with the allocator's own criteria, so the universe is not pre-filtered by someone else's approved list. Then hand the shortlist to the consultant for the operational diligence the allocator cannot do alone, and pay for the diligence rather than the recommendation. For a manager, the conclusion is the mirror image: the database listing is table stakes, and the consultant relationship is the distribution channel. The manager who is not on the consultant's approved list is invisible to half the market, however good the returns. And for anyone in the business of manager data, the missing product is now obvious. The database that verifies returns against administrator records, flags survivorship bias explicitly, and covers the emerging managers the incumbents miss will own the next decade of manager research.
